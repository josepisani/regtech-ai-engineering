# Day 4 — publishing checklist: what you owe once this is public

*2026-09-02. Checked against the consolidated AI Act (Reg. (EU) 2024/1689 as at
27.07.2026). Not legal advice — this is a working checklist, not an opinion.*

---

## Why any of this applies: publishing makes you a provider

Two definitions decide it, and the commonly-checked one is the wrong one.

> **'making available on the market'** — the supply of an AI system … for
> distribution or use on the Union market **in the course of a commercial
> activity**, whether in return for payment or free of charge.

Free does not exempt you; *non-commercial* might. A portfolio demo is arguably
not a commercial activity.

But this is the limb that catches you, and it has no commercial qualifier at all:

> **'putting into service'** — the supply of an AI system for first use directly
> to the deployer **or for own use** in the Union for its intended purpose.

Deploy it and use it and you have put it into service. **You are its provider.**

## What tier the tool itself is

Run your own ladder over it:

| Gate | Answer |
|---|---|
| Art. 2 scope | applies — put into service in the EU |
| Art. 5 prohibited | no |
| Annex III | **no.** The only near-miss is point 8(a), which requires use "by a judicial authority or on their behalf". Every other entry concerns decisions about natural persons; this classifies **systems**, not people. |
| Annex I | no — not a safety component of a regulated product |
| Art. 50 | **yes** — see below |

**Verdict: limited_risk.** Nothing from Chapter III applies: no risk management
system, no technical documentation, no conformity assessment, no CE marking, no
Art. 49 registration, no post-market monitoring. Chapter V GPAI obligations sit
with the model provider, not with you.

## The five things to ship on Day 4

### 1. Say it is AI — Art. 50(1)

> Providers shall ensure that AI systems intended to interact directly with
> natural persons are designed and developed in such a way that the natural
> persons concerned are informed that they are interacting with an AI system,
> unless this is obvious…

Arguably obvious from the app's framing. Not worth arguing when one line settles
it. Put it next to the input box, not buried in a footer.

### 2. Mark the output as generated — Art. 50(2), cautious reading

The obligation binds providers of systems "generating synthetic … text content",
and is disapplied where the system performs "an assistive function for standard
editing" or does "not substantially alter the input data … or the semantics
thereof".

`rationale` and `gaps` are model-written prose. That is not *altering* the input;
it is generating new text about it, so the exemption does not obviously rescue
you. Comply: add a machine-readable marker to the record, e.g.
`"ai_generated": true` alongside `ruleset_version`. One field.

### 3. The disclaimer — professional liability, NOT the AI Act

**This is the item most likely to be forgotten and the one with the largest
downside.** The tool tells people what their legal obligations are. Nothing in
the AI Act requires a disclaimer for a limited-risk system; the exposure is
ordinary professional liability, and it is why both reference tools carry one.

The Commission's own checker disclaims on three points, and that shape is the
one to copy — in your own words, not theirs:

1. the results are **for information only**;
2. they are **not legal advice**, and a legal professional should be consulted
   for a specific situation;
3. the output **does not represent any official or authoritative assessment** of
   the reader's situation or obligations.

Draft — put it on the page *and* in the README, and keep it above the fold:

> **This is not legal advice.** This tool produces a first-pass, machine-generated
> classification to help structure an AI system inventory. It is not a legal
> opinion, it is not a substitute for reading Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, and it
> does not represent the assessment of any authority, of the CSSF, or of any
> firm. Verify every classification against the text and take professional
> advice before relying on it. Outputs are generated by an AI system and can be
> wrong.

Add, once it exists, the honest version of the accuracy story: *"Measured
accuracy on a hand-labelled set of N descriptions is X — see the eval section."*
A disclaimer plus a real number reads as competence. A disclaimer alone reads as
cover.

### 4. Do not store what people paste — GDPR

**The largest real risk here, and it is not the AI Act.** A public paste box
means strangers paste whatever they like, including CVs, DDQ answers and named
individuals. At that point you are a controller under the GDPR.

Fix it in code, not in a warning:

- do not log request bodies — check the Cloud Run logging config, not just your
  own code;
- log only what you need (timestamp, latency, token counts, tier) and never the
  input text;
- check what your model provider's API settings do with request data and say so;
- state it plainly on the page: *"This tool does not store the text you paste."*

"We do not retain inputs" is both the compliant answer and the right answer in
front of a Conducting Officer.

### 5. One README line on AI literacy — Art. 4

You are the provider and the deployer. Art. 4 asks for measures to support AI
literacy proportionate to context; for a one-person tool that is a sentence
saying who built it, what model it calls, and what it can and cannot do.

## Checklist

- [ ] "Classifications are generated by an AI system" beside the input — Art. 50(1)
- [ ] `"ai_generated": true` in the record — Art. 50(2)
- [ ] Disclaimer on the page and in the README — professional liability
- [ ] Inputs not logged, and said so — GDPR; verify in the Cloud Run config
- [ ] AI literacy / "what this is and is not" line in the README — Art. 4
- [ ] Custom domain mapped — §10 of the spec, filtering
- [ ] Static worked example + screen recording in the README — §10 of the spec

## One thing worth doing on the day

**Run the tool over its own description.** If v2 returns `applies` / `provider` /
`art25_trigger: none` / `limited_risk` / `Article 50(1)`, that is a working demo,
a self-consistency check, and the best possible answer to "have you thought
about your own compliance?" — in one screenshot.

If it comes back `minimal_risk`, you have found a real classification bug, which
is worth more than the demo.
