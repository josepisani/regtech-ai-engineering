# Project 1 — EU AI Act System Inventory & Vendor AI Classifier

**Spec, v1 — 2026-08-31 (Day 2)**

---

## 1. What it is

One messy vendor or system description in. One validated JSON record out.

The record is a row in an AI system inventory: what the system is, what role we hold in relation to it, which risk tier applies under the EU AI Act, which obligations follow, what the description failed to tell us, and how sure the classification is.

**User:** a Conducting Officer, risk function or compliance officer at a Luxembourg ManCo who has been handed forty vendor descriptions and a blank inventory template.

**The ten-second test:** paste the text of a vendor's product page or DDQ answer into a box, press classify, get a defensible row.

**What it is not:** legal advice, and not a substitute for reading the regulation. It is a *first-pass triage* that turns forty documents into forty structured rows, of which the ten interesting ones get human attention.

---

## 2. The output contract

```python
class Classification(BaseModel):
    system_name: str
    provider_name: str | None
    system_purpose: str

    our_role: Role                      # provider | deployer | importer | distributor | unclear
    risk_tier: RiskTier                 # prohibited | high_risk | limited_risk
                                        # | minimal_risk | insufficient_information
    is_gpai: bool
    gpai_note: str | None

    legal_basis: list[str]              # e.g. ["Annex III 4(a)", "Article 5(1)(f)"]
    rationale: str
    obligations: list[str]              # derived in Python, not by the model
    gaps: list[str]
    confidence: Confidence              # low | medium | high
    classified_at: date
    ruleset_version: str
```

### Field-by-field, and why

**`system_name`, `provider_name`, `system_purpose`** — the extraction step. `provider_name` is nullable because plenty of internal descriptions never name the vendor; `system_name` and `system_purpose` are required, because if the text doesn't support even those two, the answer is `insufficient_information` and the gaps list says so.

**`our_role`** — required, with `unclear` as an explicit value. This is the field that stops the obligations list from being fiction. A ManCo buying a vendor tool is a **deployer**; a ManCo that fine-tunes a model and puts its own name on it can become a **provider** and inherit an entirely different obligation set. Same system, same tier, opposite consequences. Getting the tier right and the role wrong produces a confidently wrong answer, which is the failure mode this project exists to avoid.

**`risk_tier`** — five values, four legal and one honest. `insufficient_information` is not a tier in the regulation and does not pretend to be; it is the answer "this description does not let anyone classify this", and it is a **finding**, not an error. Treating it as a tier keeps the field non-null so every downstream consumer has one code path, and makes refusal countable in the eval.

**`is_gpai` + `gpai_note`** — a flag, not a tier, because GPAI is an orthogonal axis: a general-purpose model can sit underneath a minimal-risk system or a high-risk one. The note carries the qualifier (whether it looks like a systemic-risk model, whether we're using it as-is or building on it) without inventing a sixth tier.

**`legal_basis`** — a list of citations, separate from the prose. Separate because it is the field the eval can score exactly: "did it cite Annex III 4(a)" is checkable; "was the rationale good" is not. This is also the field that will earn the demo its credibility with a regulator-facing audience.

**`rationale`** — two or three sentences, in the model's words. For humans. Never scored automatically.

**`obligations`** — **derived in Python from `(risk_tier, our_role, is_gpai)`, not generated by the model.** Once the tier and role are fixed, the obligations are a lookup table in the regulation. Asking a model to recite a table it can get subtly wrong, when a dict cannot, is paying for a hallucination risk you don't have to take. This is also the honest answer to "how do you stop it making things up": for this field, the model is not consulted.

**`gaps`** — the questions to put back to the vendor. Free text, one per item, each phrased as a missing fact rather than an accusation: *"The description does not state whether outputs are used to rank or score individual candidates."* On an `insufficient_information` verdict this list is the product.

**`confidence`** — three levels, not a number. A model that reports `0.87` is reporting a number that was never calibrated against anything; putting that decimal in front of a risk committee implies a precision that does not exist. Three levels can be defended and can be checked against your labels on Day 13. (There is also a hard technical reason — see §5.)

**`classified_at` + `ruleset_version`** — every row records the date it was produced and which version of the rules produced it. See §4: the timeline moved this year, and a row classified under the old one is not wrong so much as *stale*, which is a different thing and needs to be visible.

## 3. Tiers and how to decide them

The model is asked to work down this ladder and stop at the first hit. Order matters — a system can satisfy more than one description, and the ladder resolves it.

| Tier | Test | Financial-sector examples |
|---|---|---|
| **prohibited** | Article 5 practices | Emotion recognition on employees in the workplace. Social scoring. Untargeted scraping of facial images. |
| **high_risk** | Annex III listed use, or Annex I safety component | **Creditworthiness assessment (Annex III 5(b))**, **life & health insurance risk assessment and pricing (5(c))**, CV screening and recruitment (4(a)), employee monitoring/promotion decisions (4(b)), biometric ID (1). |
| **limited_risk** | Article 50 transparency duties | Client-facing chatbot. Anything generating synthetic text, image or audio. Emotion recognition or biometric categorisation outside the prohibited cases. |
| **minimal_risk** | Everything else | Portfolio analytics, NAV anomaly detection, document summarisation, meeting notes, code assistants. |
| **insufficient_information** | The description does not establish the use, the affected persons, or our role | — |

**Two domain notes worth writing down, because they're where a generic classifier gets it wrong:**

- Most AI in fund management is **minimal risk**. An investment-analytics or reconciliation tool is not high-risk merely because it is important, expensive, or regulated by someone else. Financial-services regulation and AI Act tiering are different axes; a system can be critical under DORA and minimal under the AI Act. The classifier must resist the pull toward high-risk — that pull is the main source of false positives in this domain, and few-shot examples are the lever that controls it.
- The genuinely high-risk cases in a ManCo are mostly about **people, not portfolios**: recruitment, HR, worker management, and — where the group does it — credit and insurance decisions on natural persons.

## 4. Timeline — verify before Day 4 ships

This matters enough to be a field in the schema. The Digital Omnibus moved the high-risk dates:

| | Original | After the Omnibus |
|---|---|---|
| Prohibitions (Art. 5), AI literacy (Art. 4) | 2 Feb 2025 | unchanged, **in force** |
| GPAI obligations (Ch. V) | 2 Aug 2025 | unchanged, **in force** |
| **Art. 50 transparency** | 2 Aug 2026 | **unchanged — live now** |
| **Annex III high-risk** | 2 Aug 2026 | **2 Dec 2027** |
| **Annex I embedded high-risk** | 2 Aug 2027 | **2 Aug 2028** |

Also added: a new prohibition covering AI that generates non-consensual intimate imagery or CSAM, with a transition to 2 Dec 2026.

**Adoption status is the open item.** Two sources disagree: one describes a provisional political agreement of 6 May 2026 not yet formally adopted; another reports Parliament endorsement on 16 June and Council approval on 29 June 2026, without confirming Official Journal publication. **Check EUR-Lex directly before Day 4**, and put the answer in the README. A classifier that states its ruleset date and cites the correct timeline is more credible than one that quietly assumes; a demo that says "classified under the post-Omnibus timeline, ruleset v1, 2026-08-31" is doing something most compliance tools don't.

The product consequence: **the delay changes the sequence, not the obligations.** A high-risk finding today is a 2027 project, not a today problem — but Article 50 transparency is live *now*. Two different urgencies out of one classification, and the output should make that legible.

## 5. How the output is guaranteed — verified against the installed SDK

The plan assumed forced tool use plus retry. The actual API in `anthropic==1.0.0` (the version in your `.venv`) offers something stronger, and it changes the design:

- **`client.messages.parse(..., output_format=Classification)`** takes the Pydantic class directly, sends its JSON Schema, and returns `response.parsed_output` as an instance of the class. Confirmed in the installed source at `resources/messages/messages.py:1113`. Schema conformance is enforced during generation, not hoped for.
- **Not everything in the schema survives.** `anthropic/lib/_parse/_transform.py` **strips `minimum`, `maximum`, `minLength`, `maxLength` and `pattern` out of the schema and appends them to the field's description as text.** They become a hint to the model, not a constraint on it.

  That is the hard reason `confidence` is an enum rather than a float. A `Field(ge=0.0, le=1.0)` would have looked enforced and would not have been. An `enum` is in the supported subset and genuinely is. *(Worth re-reading that sentence — it is the kind of detail that separates code that works from code that appears to.)*
- **Two documented ways conformance still breaks:** `stop_reason == "refusal"` and `stop_reason == "max_tokens"`. In both cases the response may not match the schema. So validation and retry still earn their place — narrower than originally planned, but not gone.

So `toolkit/structured.py` wraps: call → check `stop_reason` → validate → on failure, retry feeding back the exact error → after N attempts, raise honestly. It keeps working if you point it at a provider without native structured outputs, which is the portability argument for building it rather than calling `.parse()` inline everywhere.

**Determinism comes from the grammar and the schema. Not from `temperature`** — which Sonnet 5 and the rest of the 5-series reject outright, as `llm_probe.py` already handles.

## 6. Scope for Day 2 — and what is deliberately not in it

**In:** the schema. The obligations lookup. One model call producing a valid `Classification`. `toolkit/structured.py`. 15–20 synthetic vendor descriptions, hand-labelled by you.

**Out, on purpose:**
- No UI (Day 3), no deployment (Day 4).
- No decomposition into extract → classify → derive. **One call and a good schema first.** Split only when a traced failure justifies it — splitting speculatively turns a two-hour build into two days.
- No retrieval over the AI Act text. The tier rules are in the prompt and the obligations in a dict. Project 2 is where the corpus arrives.
- No Annex I product-safety branch beyond flagging it. Out of scope for a ManCo.

## 7. Failure semantics

Two failures that must never be confused:

| | What happened | What the system does |
|---|---|---|
| **Model failure** | Invalid output, refusal, truncation | Retry with the error fed back. After N attempts, **raise**. Never return a partial record, never default the tier. |
| **Inadequate input** | The description genuinely doesn't say enough | Return a valid record, `risk_tier = insufficient_information`, gaps populated, confidence `low`. |

The second is a product feature. "This vendor's description does not state whether the tool scores individual candidates, so it cannot be classified — ask them these three questions" is a more useful output than a confident tier, because it is actionable.

## 8. The eval hook

Day 2 produces 15–20 synthetic descriptions, hand-labelled by you, deliberately spanning: obvious minimal, obvious high-risk, genuinely borderline, an Article 5 case, a GPAI case, and **three or four that are under-specified on purpose** — those are the ones that test whether it refuses honestly or bluffs.

Stored as `data/vendor_descriptions.jsonl` (the text) and `data/labels.jsonl` (your labels), separate files so the descriptions can be shown in a demo without leaking the answers.

**Nothing built on Day 2 measures correctness.** The schema guarantees shape. Only these labels will ever measure whether the answers are right, and Day 13 depends entirely on their quality. Label them carefully; where you hesitate, write down why — that hesitation is the most valuable annotation in the file.

## 9. Day 2 gate

1. A messy vendor description returns a schema-conformant `Classification` **every time**, across all 15–20 descriptions.
2. Under-specified descriptions return `insufficient_information` with populated gaps, **not** an invented tier.
3. `toolkit/structured.py` is imported by the classifier, not merely written.

*Not in the gate: accuracy. That is Week 3, and pretending otherwise on Day 2 is how people ship confident nonsense.*

## 10. Deployment constraints — the corporate perimeter (added 2026-09-02)

The demo runs on Cloud Run and calls the Anthropic API. That is correct for the
portfolio and wrong for the actual user, and the spec should say so rather than
discover it in a meeting.

### 10.1 The constraint

Most Luxembourg banks and financial entities run URL filtering and DLP on
outbound traffic, and commonly block pages with embedded AI outright. Two
separate failures follow, and they need separate answers:

1. **The site is unreachable.** A `*.run.app` hostname is a shared Google
   domain that filtering products routinely bucket as uncategorised or
   cloud-hosting. The user sees a block page.
2. **Even if reachable, the paste is blocked.** DLP inspects what leaves the
   network. A vendor's public product page is marketing text and is probably
   fine; a DDQ answer is internal and probably is not.

### 10.2 Who is actually affected

| Audience | Affected? | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Hiring managers, recruiters | No — phone or personal laptop | The demo works. The ten-second test stands. |
| A contact you show it to at their desk | **Yes** | They see a block page and you learn nothing |
| A real ManCo user | **Yes** | The hosted demo is not a usable product for them |

This does not invalidate Project 1 — §2 of START-HERE already decided not to
launch the SaaS. It invalidates the assumption that the deployed demo doubles as
a product, and it changes how Day 4's "show it to one person in your network"
step has to be run.

### 10.3 Mitigations that are in scope

- **Map a custom domain** to the Cloud Run service. Does not defeat DLP; does
  get the URL out of the worst filtering category, and reads better on a CV.
- **Record a 20–30 second screen capture** of a real classification and put it
  in the README. Someone on a locked-down laptop can watch it when they cannot
  reach the app. Highest value per minute on this list.
- **Put a worked example in the README as static text** — input description,
  output record — so the tool can be understood with nothing executed.

### 10.4 The architecture answer — and why ours already survives it

**The realistic deployment target is the firm's own model.** Banks and financial
entities in this market generally do run AI internally: open-weight models,
self-hosted, heavily guard-railed. So the integration question is not "will they
let you call Anthropic" — the answer is no — it is **"can this run against our
internal model endpoint?"**

Four layers, weakest to strongest:

1. Custom domain, EU hosting.
2. **Deploy inside the perimeter.** It is a container. The only remaining
   egress is the model call.
3. **Keep the model call in the EU** — e.g. Vertex AI in `europe-west1`. This is
   a data-residency argument for the Gemini work, separate from the
   multi-provider credibility argument.
4. **Call their internal model.** Self-hosted open-weight model behind an
   OpenAI-compatible endpoint (vLLM, Ollama, LiteLLM are the usual shapes).
   Zero egress. This is the one that actually matches how these firms operate.

**Two design decisions already made pay off here, and neither was made for this
reason:**

- **Obligations are a Python dict, not model output.** Swap to a weaker model
  and the obligation list does not degrade at all, because no model ever
  generated it. Accuracy of the *tier* drops; the citations cannot rot.
- **`toolkit/structured.py` wraps call → check `stop_reason` → validate →
  retry-with-error → raise.** Against Anthropic that wrapper is partly
  redundant, because `messages.parse` enforces conformance with a grammar. A
  self-hosted open-weight model behind a plain endpoint has **no such
  guarantee** — there the validate-and-retry loop is the only thing standing
  between the app and malformed JSON. The portability argument for building the
  wrapper was speculative on Day 2. This is the concrete case that justifies it.

**What does degrade:** classification accuracy on a smaller model, by an unknown
amount. That is measurable, and measuring it is a Week 3 job — running the
labelled set against a second, weaker model gives a real number for "what does
it cost you to keep this inside your own walls". That comparison is a portfolio
asset in its own right.

### 10.5 What to write in the README

One short section, honestly worded: the hosted demo is for evaluation; the
deployment path for a regulated firm is in-perimeter with the firm's own model;
the classification core is provider-agnostic by construction and the obligation
lookup does not depend on the model at all.

## 11. Output contract v2 — the gates (2026-09-02)

§2 above describes contract v1. This section supersedes it. `RULESET_VERSION` is
now `v2-2026-09-02-reg-2024-1689-consolidated-20260727`.

**What changed and why:** v1 asked the model for one judgement — the tier. The
Act does not work that way. It is a sequence of gates, each of which can end the
analysis, and v1 had no field for four of them. Every gap below produced a
confidently wrong answer in a case a ManCo actually meets.

| Gate | Article | Field | Type |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Does the Act apply? | Art. 2 | `act_applies` | `applies` / `excluded` / `unclear` |
| | | `exclusion_ground` | 5 grounds + `none` |
| 2. Stated role | Art. 3 | `our_role` | unchanged |
| 3. Escalated role? | Art. 25 | `art25_trigger` | 3 triggers + `none` / `unclear` |
| 4. Tier | Arts. 5, 6 | `risk_tier` | unchanged |
| 5. Derogated? | Art. 6(3) | `annex_iii_derogation` | 4 limbs + `none` |
| 6. Profiling override | Art. 6(3) final subpara | `performs_profiling` | bool |

Derived in Python, never by the model:

- **`effective_role`** — `Classification.derive_effective_role()` applies
  Art. 25(1). Deliberately narrow: escalation only on the high-risk path,
  because all three Art. 25(1) triggers are tied to high-risk systems. The
  stated role is kept in `our_role` and never overwritten, so the delta is
  auditable and Day 13 can score "did it spot the escalation" separately from
  "did it get the role right".
- **`obligations`** — `obligations_for()` now applies the gates in the Act's
  order and each can end the function: Art. 2 scope → Art. 6(3)/(4) derogation →
  the tier table → an Art. 25 note explaining why the role changed.

### Three distinctions the schema now keeps apart

1. **`excluded` vs `insufficient_information`.** "The Act does not reach this"
   and "we cannot tell what this is" are different findings. v1 collapsed the
   first into `minimal_risk`, which is a stronger claim than either.
2. **A carve-out vs a derogation.** Both end in "not high-risk". A **carve-out**
   is written into the Annex III entry — Annex III 5(b) excepts *"AI systems
   used for the purpose of detecting financial fraud"* — so the system was never
   in Annex III, `annex_iii_derogation` stays `none`, and no Art. 6(4) duty
   arises. A **derogation** under Art. 6(3) lifts out a system that *is* in
   Annex III, and creates a documentation and registration duty of its own.
   For a ManCo the fraud carve-out is the most valuable sentence in Annex III:
   AML and transaction-monitoring tools that score natural persons are not
   high-risk on the 5(b) limb.
3. **Profiling beats every limb.** Art. 6(3) final subparagraph: an Annex III
   system *"shall always be considered to be high-risk where the AI system
   performs profiling of natural persons"*. `obligations_for()` checks this
   before the limbs and, where both are set, emits an explicit line saying the
   derogation does not apply.

### Also corrected while in the file

`UNIVERSAL` (Art. 4) is now gated on role and reworded. Art. 4(1) binds
**providers and deployers**, not importers or distributors, and says *take
measures to support the development of* AI literacy — with an express statement,
added by the Omnibus, that it does not require any specific level to be
guaranteed for any individual. v1 said "ensure staff have sufficient AI
literacy" and applied it to everyone, which was stronger than the law on both
counts.

### Cost of the change

The prompt now carries five worked examples instead of three, and the schema has
16 required fields instead of 11. That is real input cost on every call — which
is what prompt caching on Day 3 is for, since the examples never change between
calls.

### One judgement to check before labelling

Few-shot example 5 (`InterviewScribe`) encodes a decision that is defensible but
arguable: a transcription-and-structuring tool used inside a recruitment process
is treated as within Annex III 4(a) and then derogated under Art. 6(3)(d) as a
preparatory task, with `performs_profiling` false. **You are the expert labeller
— check that you agree before you label.** If you disagree, the example is
teaching the model the opposite of your ground truth, and the eval will measure
that disagreement rather than the model's ability.

The alternative was to ship no derogation example at all, in which case the
field would almost certainly always come back `none` and the whole gate would be
decorative. A contested example you have reviewed beats a gate that never fires.
